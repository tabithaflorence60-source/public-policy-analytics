# Public Goods and Externalities

Public goods and externalities explain why individually rational choices can produce socially undesirable outcomes. They are central to environmental, climate, infrastructure, and energy policy.

## 1. The analytical benchmark

The welfare-economics benchmark begins with a competitive market in which prices reflect the relevant costs and benefits of production and consumption. When important costs or benefits fall on people outside the transaction, the private market outcome can diverge from the socially preferred outcome.

## 2. Externalities

A **negative externality** occurs when an activity imposes costs on others that are not fully reflected in the market price. Emissions from electricity generation and transportation are standard examples.

A **positive externality** occurs when an activity creates benefits for others that are not fully captured by the actor making the investment. Examples can include knowledge spillovers, some energy-efficiency investments, and early deployment of technologies that help lower future technology costs.

A useful policy distinction is:

- **private marginal cost/benefit** — experienced by the decision-maker
- **external marginal cost/benefit** — experienced by others
- **social marginal cost/benefit** — the combined effect

## 3. Public goods and collective action

Public goods are generally characterized by non-rivalry and difficulty of exclusion. Climate stability is a useful global example: actors can benefit from emissions reductions even if they do not themselves bear the cost of reducing emissions.

This creates collective-action and free-rider problems. The problem is especially important in climate policy because decisions are made across households, firms, cities, regions, states, and international institutions.

## 4. Common-pool resources

Common-pool resources combine difficulty of exclusion with rivalry in use. The core policy problem is not simply whether government or markets should control the resource, but what institutional arrangements create credible rules, monitoring, cooperation, and accountability.

## 5. Policy responses

Typical responses include:

- command-and-control regulation
- emissions taxes or other price instruments
- cap-and-trade or quantity instruments
- subsidies and tax incentives
- public investment
- information and disclosure
- collective or polycentric governance arrangements

Economic efficiency is one criterion, but policy analysis should also evaluate distribution, administrative capacity, political feasibility, legitimacy, and implementation.

## 6. Applied energy-policy questions

- How should the social cost of emissions be incorporated into electricity or transportation policy?
- When are renewable-energy subsidies justified by learning or technology spillovers?
- How should grid reliability be treated when reliability benefits extend beyond individual market transactions?
- Which climate actions are best coordinated nationally, and which can be pursued through multiple centers of decision-making?

## 7. Quantitative application

A future quantitative application could compare private and social costs, estimate equilibrium differences, and examine how policy instruments change outcomes. Any R implementation will be added only after the model assumptions, equations, and code have been checked and documented.

## 8. Research connection

This topic supports analysis of emissions, renewable-energy incentives, electricity reliability, transportation externalities, and collective-action problems in energy transitions. It also provides a bridge from welfare economics to institutional and governance analysis.
